# Bottlenecks, Automation, and Self-Serving Bias

Timestamp: 2026-11-12 14:23 MST

## Previous prompt

analyze the following text: "The other 60% is the bit that was always hard: understanding the problem, agreeing what to build, reviewing
what comes back."

That same law says that if there is a part that is "always hard" then it is a well-known roadblock.

So why assume that it will always be hard?

Because that favors your continued employment.

Which is called "user bias".

## Analysis

The text makes a useful challenge, but overstates its conclusion.

Its strongest point is that **a known bottleneck should be a target for improvement, not treated as a permanent limit.** Understanding
requirements, deciding what to build, and reviewing results can themselves benefit from better tools and processes. The quoted “60%” also
needs evidence: whose work, measured how, under what conditions?

However, the argument makes three leaps:

- **Past difficulty becomes future impossibility.** “Was always hard” describes historical experience; it doesn’t necessarily mean “will
  always be hard.”
- **A known problem becomes a solvable problem.** Recognizing a roadblock helps direct effort, but doesn’t establish how much we can reduce
  it.
- **An assumption becomes proof of motive.** Continued employment could influence someone’s judgment, but benefiting from a claim doesn’t
  demonstrate that self-interest caused it.

“User bias” is also imprecise here. The allegation is closer to **self-serving bias or motivated reasoning**—and would need supporting
evidence.

A stronger version would be:

> “Those activities have historically been difficult, but that doesn’t establish that their cost will remain fixed. Treating today’s
> bottlenecks as permanent understates the potential for further automation. People whose roles depend on those activities may have an
> incentive to do so, but the claim should be judged on evidence.”
